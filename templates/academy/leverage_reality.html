Leverage Reality — what leverage really changes education · hypothetical positions only · not advice
Educational tool. Nothing here recommends leverage, sizes a position for you, or places an order. Liquidation prices below come from a simplified theoretical model; real exchanges use tiered margins and mark prices, so real outcomes differ.
Account equity
Your money: wallet balance plus or minus the open positions' unrealised profit or loss.
Margin
The part of your money locked as collateral for a position. Margin × leverage = the position's size.
Notional exposure
The full value of the position. Profit and loss move with THIS, not with the margin.
Liquidation
When equity behind a position falls to the maintenance requirement, the exchange closes it — usually at a worse price.
Leverage does not make a trade more likely to be right. It makes the same price move matter more: at 20×, a 1 % move is 20 % of your margin, and a move of roughly 5 % against you can wipe it out. It changes exposure and the distance to liquidation — it does not create an edge.

Why the liquidation price is not where you get out
The liquidation price is the level at which the exchange starts closing your position. In a fast market the order fills wherever buyers or sellers are, often well past that level, and a liquidation fee is charged. Exchanges watch a mark price (a smoothed fair price), not the last trade, and the maintenance rate rises in tiers as the position grows. Cross margin moves the liquidation point further away by putting your whole account behind the position — which means a liquidation then takes the whole account.
Why a 95 % win rate can still lose everything
A DCA ladder that takes small profits wins most cycles: price usually comes back a little. The rare cycle where price keeps falling fills every step, piles up exposure, and ends in liquidation — and one liquidation can erase hundreds of small wins. Screenshots of winning days show the first part only. Judge a strategy by its worst case and how often it happens, never by its win rate.
Funding — the cost of holding a perpetual
Every few hours, perpetual-futures longs and shorts pay each other a funding rate. When it is positive, longs pay shorts; when negative, shorts pay longs. It is charged on the full exposure, so leverage multiplies it, and it changes constantly — a positive rate today is not guaranteed tomorrow. See the historical record in the Funding Carry Monitor.
